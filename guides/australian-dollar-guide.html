Australian Dollar Guide: USD to AUD and Travelling Down Under

What moves the Aussie dollar

The Australian dollar is a commodity currency, closely linked to the prices of iron ore, coal, and other resources Australia exports, and to the health of the Chinese economy, which buys much of them. When commodity prices rise and China grows strongly, the Aussie dollar strengthens; when they fall, it weakens. The currency is also sensitive to global risk sentiment, rising when investors feel confident and falling when they seek safety in the US dollar.

In recent years the USD to AUD rate has traded roughly between 1.45 and 1.60, meaning one US dollar buys about 1.50 Australian dollars. The Reserve Bank of Australia's interest rate decisions also move the currency, though usually less dramatically than commodity prices. For anyone converting money, the rate is volatile enough that the timing of a large conversion can matter, but provider costs still dominate for most amounts.

Cheapest ways to convert USD to AUD

For transfers to Australia, online specialist services are usually cheapest, with total costs under one percent and delivery within one to two days. Australian banks charge two to four percent, and US banks are similar, so both are expensive for anything beyond a small amount. A multi-currency account that holds Australian dollars is useful for repeat travellers and for Australians living abroad who send money home regularly.

For spending in Australia, a fee-free card is the best option. Australia is one of the most card-friendly countries in the world, with contactless payment accepted almost everywhere, from city cafes to rural petrol stations. You need very little cash, though small markets and some rural businesses may still prefer it. Withdrawing Australian dollars from an ATM is reasonable, but check your bank's foreign ATM fee first.

Money tips for Australia

Australian banknotes are polymer, waterproof, and come in bright colours, with the five dollar note being the smallest. Prices include tax, so the price you see is the price you pay, unlike in the United States, and tipping is not expected, though a small tip for exceptional service is appreciated. Keep an eye on the exchange rate if you are travelling for a long time, since a five percent move in the Aussie dollar changes the cost of a long trip noticeably, and converting a larger sum when the rate is favourable can save real money.

Putting It Into Practice

Before travelling to Australia, check that your card charges no foreign transaction fee, since you will use it for almost everything. Learn that prices include tax and that tipping is not expected, which simplifies budgeting, and keep an eye on the exchange rate during a long trip, since a five percent move changes the cost noticeably. If you visit regularly, a multi-currency account holding Australian dollars lets you convert when the rate is favourable. Keep your receipts for larger purchases, since visitors can sometimes claim back the goods and services tax on goods taken home under the tourist refund scheme. Check the scheme's rules before you shop, because it has a minimum spend and requires the goods to be carried in hand luggage when you leave. A little preparation goes a long way in a country as large and varied as Australia.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many Australian dollars do I get for 100 US dollars?

At 1.52 AUD per USD, about 152 Australian dollars at the mid-market rate, less after any spread or fee.

Is Australia cashless?

Almost. Contactless cards are accepted nearly everywhere, so you need very little cash.

What moves the Australian dollar?

Commodity prices, especially iron ore, Chinese demand, and global risk sentiment.
